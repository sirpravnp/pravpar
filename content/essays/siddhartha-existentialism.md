---
title: Herman Hesse’s Siddhartha in the Lens of Existentialism: A Syncretic Journey towards the Absurd and Beyond
date: 2024-05-17
---

Eastern philosophical influences on existentialism began most publicly – and somewhat ironically – in the form of Arthur Schopenhauer, a noted pessimist, whose proposed solution to the utter suffering he ascribed to the human condition was rooted in Indian traditions of asceticism. Schopenhauerian pessimism, though on the opposite ideological extreme as conventional existentialist notions, was of strong value to Nietzsche, whose declaration of humanity’s will-to-power supplanted Schopenhauer’s will-to-live. The Indian thought introduced by Schopenhauer into Western consciousness further proved to inspire Herman Hesse, a German novelist and poet whose literary utilization of Schopenhauerian motifs, along with their Indian predecessors, remain influential in the modern day. Hesse’s *Siddhartha*, set in the India of Gautama Buddha, follows the journey of its main character through the four *ashramas*, or Hindu stages of life. Though undoubtedly inspired by Schopenhauer, *Siddhartha* surpasses the thralls of pessimism, rather joining the ranks of existentialist literature, explained in the development of Siddhartha’s worldview from Schopenhauerian asceticism, to Nietzche’s will to power and Sartre’s existentialism, finally settling on a modified form of Camus’ Sisyphean absurdism.

*Siddhartha*, aptly named after the novel’s titular character, is a portmanteau of the Sanskrit words *Siddha*, meaning achievement, and *Artha*, representing meaning. It is evident, therefore, that the novel’s title foreshadows the existential journey that is to be exposed in its contents. The characterization of an existentialist work – most briefly summarized by Sartre’s declaration of *l'existence précède l'essence* – begins in the ennui of its characters. Siddhartha is described by Hesse as one who “knew how to recognize Atman within the depth of his being, indestructible, at one with the universe”, one who “made everybody happy”; yet, “Siddhartha himself … \[is\] not happy” (Hesse 2). The “restlessness of the soul”, the “seeds of discontent within” Siddhartha satisfy the conditions of the existentialist narrative, the same apparent disease of the mind that torments Sartre’s Roquentin (Hesse 2). Camus, in his exposition of the plight of Sisyphus, describes the progenitor of this fundamental dread, declaring “if this myth is tragic, that is because its hero is conscious … \[life\] is tragic only at the rare moments when it becomes conscious” (Kauffman 314). In its expository chapters, it is made clear that Siddhartha is aware of his conditions, of the eternal meaninglessness of the practices of ancient India’s learned *brahmins* (the caste to which Siddhartha and his family belong to), declaring that “the ablutions were good, but they were water; they did not wash sins away, they did not relieve the distressed heart” (Hesse 3). Yet, unlike Sartre’s Roquentin, Siddhartha is aware of a certain philosophical baseline that is codified in the Upanishads, of the seemingly eternal relationship between the “Self”, the source of his existential inquiry, and the broader universe, the hidden essence termed “Brahman”. It is here, in this void of existential meaning, the chasm that separates the Self from this ever-elusive Brahman, that Siddhartha’s journey truly begins.

Embarking on his quest as a *samana*, Siddhartha leaves the boundaries of his limited existence, traveling into the forest voluntarily as a beggar. The Sanskrit word *samana* originates from the root *śram*, to toil. These renunciates, aware of the world and its pains, serve as a societal contrast to the Brahmins, whose knowledge of monism is limited to ritual. Notably, the same samanic traditions that underlied Buddhism were of great influence to Schopenhauer, whose “road to salvation” is paved by a renunciation of the “will to live” (Ñāṇājīvako). The Samanas have “one single goal – to become empty, to become empty of thirst, desire, dreams, pleasure and sorrow – to let the Self die” (Hesse 11). It is evident that the Upanishadic notion of the Self compares favorably to Schopenhauer’s will-to-live, the rejection of the fundamentally biological strife, the “one root … \[from which\] the phenomenal world, diversity of all beings, individuality, egoism, hatred, wickedness” spring (Ñāṇājīvako). Yet, Hesse’s writings are – implicitly – equally critical of Schopenhauerian samanic concepts as they are influenced by its renunciate asceticism. Though Siddhartha learns to submerge the Self, his will to live, his efforts as a Samana are consistently trounced by this seemingly invincible foe, a force that repeatedly awakens him to the “torment of the onerous life cycle” (Hesse 12). In the perspective of Camus, Siddhartha’s path rests at the foothold of realizing eternal meaninglessness, the origins of his withdrawal from Brahminical life and his subsequent descent into asceticism. Yet, Siddhartha’s perception of the inevitability of his selfhood prevents him from free-fall into nihilism, as he begins to comprehend the foolishness of radical renunciation, describing it as a “temporary palliative against the pain and folly of life” (Hesse 13). It is important to note that Hesse’s critique of Schopenhauer’s call to destruction of the will-to-live, the desires of the Self, falls in line with that of Kierkegaard, who famously deemed Schopenhauer’s treatises as latently “eudaemonic” (*Kierkegaard’s Praise and Criticism of Schopenhauer*). Though the Samanas practice denial of pleasure in the face of eternal meaninglessness, their efforts seek to avoid the pain of life, a contradictory approach preceding Camus’ notions of physical and philosophical suicide.

Siddhartha’s first encounter with the absurd begins upon his discontentment with the Samanas. In conversation with his childhood companion, Siddhartha declares, “there is, so I believe, in the essence of everything, something we cannot call learning … and I am beginning to believe that this knowledge has no worse enemy than the man of knowledge, than learning” (Hesse 15). In the concepts of Brahman, the hidden, but united nature of existence, lies a reframing of Camus’ discussion of the inherent meaninglessness of life, codified in Hindu tradition as *samsara*. Siddhartha is aware of this truth, but is yet to accept, a poignant representation of the didactic divide between knowledge and wisdom. Though Siddhartha understands the folly of his sensory escape under the Samanas, he is unable to fully accept the unity of the Self with the eternal, with contextualizing the drive of the ephemeral will-to-live in the greater expanse of the timeless universe. The possibilities of the absurd continue to expand in the novel’s plot upon the introduction of Gotama, widely known as the Buddha, the ultimate catalyst for Siddhartha’s departure from the Samanas. In his first and only conversation with Gotama, Siddhartha describes the teachings of the exalted teacher as “linked together by cause and effect … a complete, unbroken chain, an eternal chain” (Hesse 26). Yet, Siddhartha is tormented by a breakage in the teachings of Gotama, an inconsistency through which “streams into the world of unity something strange, something new, something that was not there before and that cannot be demonstrated and proved: … \[a break through which\] the eternal and single world law breaks down again” (Hesse 26). In his torturous attempts to define his existence, Siddhartha struggles in unifying the observable universe in the model of cause and effect, stumbling upon the absurdity of his existence. Faced with this unique realization, Siddhartha finds himself at the crossroads of accepting that which he deems absurd, or to ignore the discrepancies that plague him. The many followers of the Buddha, however, are not both blessed and cursed with the prerequisite social, cultural, and mental reagents of the existentialist hero. Govinda, Siddhartha’s childhood companion, whose adherence to doctrine and fear of authority surpass his thirst for knowledge, his existential torment, is a representation of these followers, to whom the Buddha explains his teachings, designed to impart “salvation from suffering\[,\] … not to explain the world to those who are thirsty for knowledge” (Hesse 27).  In analyzing Hesse’s portrayal of Buddhist philosophy, it is important to note the language utilized in developing the contrast between the “thirsty” and the “suffering”, a similar motif developed in Sartre’s *Nausea*, in describing the majority who “have dragged out their lives in stupor and somnolence, they have married in a hurry out of impatience, and they have made children at random” (Sartre 37). For the majority whose strife for subsistence masks the seemingly eternal “sweet sickness”, Gotama’s path offers hope, a subtle introduction to the absurd without its subsequent plunging. Hesse’s questioning of the Buddha’s systems of “cause and effect” harken back to Nietzsche’s *Beyond Good and Evil*, in which he so expertly states, “it is we alone who have devised cause, sequence, for-each-other, relativity, constraint, number, law, freedom, motive, and purpose; and when we project and mix this symbol world into things as if it existed 'in itself,' we act once more as we have always acted – mythologically” (Nietzsche 29). Notably, Hesse’s use of Siddhartha as a questioner serves as a mechanism of growth, as he finally sheds the renunciation of the Samanas and the Buddha – what Nietzsche terms “*la religion de la souffrance humaine*” – for a new approach: “to leave all doctrines and all teachers … to reach … \[the\] goal alone – or die” (Nietzsche 30, Hesse 28). In the interplay between Nietzsche and Gotama lies the dichotomy between the existentialist hero and the ordinary individual, a device for Hesse to implore the broader social costs of a journey towards the absurd, perhaps best stated by the Buddha, who asks: “do you think, O Samana from afar, that it would be better for all these to relinquish the teachings and to return to the life of the world and desires?” (Hesse 28).

Leaving the grove of Gotama, Siddhartha arrives upon a new conclusion, one rejecting the notions that were previously fed to him by the Brahmins, by the Samanas, by the followers of the Buddha. Hesse’s development of Siddhartha continues to follow the ethos of Nietzsche, in his rejection of the pre-packaged “cause and effect” provided by the Buddha to his followers, in his rejection of the nihilism of the Samanas. Describing Siddhartha as “the thinker”, Hesse describes the protagonist’s internal machinations, writing, “I was seeking Brahman, Atman, I wished to destroy myself, to get away from myself, in order to find in the unknown innermost, the nucleus of all things, Atman, Life, the Divine, the Absolute. But by doing so, I lost myself on the way” (Hesse 31). These sentiments lie at the crossroads between Nietzsche’s will-to-power, Sartre’s humanist perspective of existentialism, and Camus’ absurdism. Rather than disperse the Self in meditation, Siddhartha’s commitment to self-discovery and action upon his will reflects the sentiments of the strong willed described by Nietzsche. Furthermore, the acknowledgement of the futility of defining and understanding Brahman and the nature of its inner reality in the context of the Self, Atman, serves as a profound turning point in Siddhartha’s consciousness of the universe and its incomprehensibility in the minds of humankind. No longer preoccupied with questions to which he cannot discover answers to, Siddhartha resolves to define himself, embarking upon a journey into material life, into that world which he so despised as a Samana youth. A transition is made from the Kantian perspective of *maya*, the notion of the ever elusive nature of the world, to a world that is at once beautiful and apparent, that “meaning and reality were not hidden somewhere behind things, \[but\] they were in them, in all of them” (Hesse 32).

Yet, the fruits of the absurd, when taken out of the context of the eternal, slowly foreshadow the descent of hedonism. As Siddhartha acclimates to external society, the essence of what he once remembered to be true, the ephemera of materialism in the face of eternal *samsara*, the unity of all in Brahman, was also submerged, just as the Self was suppressed among the Samanas. This partial awareness, the quasi-separation of the Self from the absurd, segregates Siddhartha from the “childish people”, those fully immersed in the world of *samsara* with limited understandings of its meaninglessness. And as the Siddhartha of old – the Brahmin’s son, the Samana – began to view his asceticism as an old robe, so does the worldly Siddhartha, with “creases and stains accumulated, and hidden in the depths, here and there already appearing, waited disillusionment and nausea” (Hesse 63). Unlike the corpus of conventionally existentialist literature, Hesse’s *Siddhartha* is perhaps the only novel to depict the constant cycle that is the existential problem. Though Siddhartha has changed his environment, his philosophies, his professions, his agony has remained constant, originating from the insatiable thirst experienced by Siddhartha, the Brahmin’s son, continuing in the overpowering “nausea” plaguing Siddhartha, the businessman, the worldly man. Once again, change is required to cope with the abysses of meaninglessness. Siddhartha’s worldly journey is a cautionary tale on the burdens of an unfettered appreciation of the absurd without an equally balancing understanding of its ephemerality, itself a form of philosophical suicide. Hesse laments the plight of this new Siddhartha, writing, “how many long years he had spent without any lofty goal, without any thirst, without any exaltation, content with small pleasures and yet never really satisfied\!” (Hesse 67). With a renewed sense of the meaninglessness of *samsara*, with the nausea of hedonism, Siddhartha moves from the artificial constructionism of philosophical suicide to the cliffs of physical suicide, wishing “passionately for oblivion, to be at rest, to be dead” (Hesse 71).

It is at this moment of despair that Siddhartha ascends to the level of Camus’ absurd man, to the level of Sisyphus, of Oedipus Rex (Camus 314). Om, the eternal sound, the representation of that which is beyond meaning, is the source of Siddhartha’s next rebirth, though to describe it as such would be to ignore his past lives, the progenitors of that absurd fate which he perceives. Despite perceiving “all forms \[as\] … transitory”, Siddhartha is still able to feel a “deep love for this flowing water and decided he would not leave it again so quickly” (Hesse 81). Camus, in his ode to Sisyphus, “the proletarian of the gods”, mentions the power of the “tragic” hero’s descent, suggesting that his acceptance of the inevitable provides the opportunity for a greater happiness (Camus 314). Similarly, the Siddhartha of new, once accepting his nausea, his past lives, his follies, understands the beauty of absurdity, the “blind love of a mother for her child, the blind foolish pride of a fond father for his only son, the blind eager strivings of a young vain woman for ornament and the admiration of men” (Hesse 106). Siddhartha and his companion Vasudeva, a wise, yet simple ferryman, therefore come to represent the syncretism of Camus’ Sisyphus, the primordial absurd man, with the Eastern philosophies of *moksha*, liberation from the meaninglessness from *samsara*. The endless river, the teacher of these two wise men, comes to represent the absurd; yet, Hesse goes beyond describing these inevitabilities as absurd, but rather as united, as “all the voices, all the goals, all the yearnings, all the sorrows, all the pleasures, all the good and evil, all of them together … \[making up\] the world” (Hesse 110).

Ultimately, Siddhartha finds himself altogether unconcerned with the meaning of the self, of Brahman, rather finding joy in the beauty of the ephemeral and the eternal; in this way Hesse’s philosophy surpasses that of Camus’ absurdism, which declares understanding of meaningless the precursor to happiness. Rather, Siddhartha’s journey compounds the absurd, not only appreciating the beauty of that which is ephemeral, but also identifying the unity within that which is eternal. More importantly, Hesse’s “monist” absurdism provides a significant clarification on the attainment of its realization, emphasizing that knowledge and wisdom is constructed by the weight of the individual’s narrative, ultimately serving as a model for the role of experience in an individual’s philosophical journey.
